## Corporation

A business organized as a legal entity separate from its owners.

Types:
* Public companies: traded in public markets
* Private Corporations: closely held by a small group of investors

Characteristics:
* Shareholders do not manage/control the corporation directly;
an elected Board of Directors monitors management on their behalf.
* Limited Liability: shareholders are not personally liable for a corporation's obligations.
* Taxation: taxed on profits; shareholders are then taxed on dividends (double taxation, not in Singapore).
* Lifespan: unlimited, as shares are transferable

Other types of business organizations:
* Sole Proprietorships: owned and controlled by an individual
* Partnerships: owned and controlled by at least two people
* Corporations: separate legal entity
* Others:
  * Limited Liability Companies (LLC) or Limited Liability Partnerships (LLP): limited liability, no double taxation.
  * Professional Corporations (PC): limited liability, however professionals can be sued for malpractice.

Limited liability:
Shareholder's maximum loss is their investment in the corporation;
personal assets are not liable for corporate obligations.

Corporate life does not depend on current owners/shareholders.
Ownership can change without terminating the corporation.

### Investment and Financing Decisions

Two categories of decisions:

* Investment Decisions: decision to invest in tangible or intangible **real assets**, also called **Capital Budgeting** Decision or Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) Decision.

* Financing Decisions: decision on how to finance the investment and how to pay for it. Use **financial assets** (stocks, bonds) to raise funds, mix of long-term debt and equity financing (called **Capital Structure**).

### Financial Manager

Chief Financial Officer (CFO) is responsible for financial policy and corporate planning.

There are two more roles under the CFO:
* Treasurer: cash management, raising capital, banking relationships
* Controller: preparing financial statements, accounting and taxes

1. Cash raised from investors (Investor -> Financial Manager)
2. Cash invested in real assets (Financial Manager -> Real Assets)
3. Cash generated by operations (Real Assets -> Cash/profits)
4. Reinvest the cash or pay dividends (Financial Manager -> Investors)

### Goals of Corporation

* Shareholders desire wealth maximization

Goal: maximize current market value of shareholders' investment.

Why not profit maximization?
* Which year's profit?
* Accounting earnings can be manipulated.

* Opportunity cost of capital: a company should only invest in a project if the project is expected to earn at least as much as shareholders could earn by investing their money somewhere else with similar risk.

### Agency Problem

Managers are agents for stockholders and are tempted to act in their own interests rather than maximizing value.

Agency cost: Value lost from agency problems or from the cost of mitigating agency problems

Solution:
Executive compensation:
* Fixed base salary
* Annual award tied to earnings / other performance measures
* Stock Options: give managers the right to buy stock at a fixed price in the future, so they have an incentive to increase stock price.
* Corporate Governance

Agency problem arises from separation of ownership and management.

Least likely: Sole proprietorship → owner = manager.

## Balance Sheet

Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity

Assets:
* Current assets: cash & securities, receivables (money customers owe company), inventories (goods company has for sale)
Tangible fixed assets:
PP&E
- Accumulated depreciation
= Net tangible fixed assets

Intangible assets:
Goodwill, patents, trademarks, etc.

Liabilities:
* Current liabilities: payables (money company owes suppliers), short-term debt
* Long-term liabilities

Equity:
* Common stock & paid-in capital
* Retained earnings: profits reinvested in the company, not paid out as dividends (ending RE = beginning RE + net income - dividends)
* Treasury stock: stock repurchased by the company

Net Working Capital (NWC) = Current Assets - Current Liabilities

Balance sheet order:
Assets are listed in descending liquidity:
Cash → Receivables → Inventory → Fixed Assets

Moving downward → less liquid.

Goodwill:
When purchase price > book/accounting value of acquired net assets,
the excess may appear as goodwill.

### Book Value vs Market Value

Book value: value of assets, liabilities, and equity as shown on the balance sheet (backward-looking, historical cost)

Market value: value of assets, liabilities, and equity as determined by the market (forward-looking, based on future cash flows)

Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP): Procedures for preparing financial statements

Equity and assets market values are usually higher than book values, 


Short-term liabilities market values should be close to book values.

Long-term market values may be higher or lower than book values

Market value is forward-looking.
Book value is historical/accounting.

Usually MV > BV for assets/equity, BUT:
Change in BV does not imply a fixed change in MV.

### The Statement of Cash Flows

Acurral Accounting: profit record income and expenses at the time of sales, not when the cash exchanges actually occurs.

Profit do not consider changes in working capital, while cash flow does.

Gross Investment in Fixed Assets = Increase in Net Fixed Assets + Depreciation

Cash Flow from Operations = Net Income + Depreciation - Addition in WC (does not include cash or short-term debt/debt due for repayment, = increase in operating current assets − increase in operating current liabilities)

Cash Flow from Investments = -CapEx + Sales of long-term assets - Other investments

Cash Flow from Financing =
+ Debt issued / increase in debt
+ Stock issued
- Debt repayment
- Dividends
- Stock repurchases
± Other financing

* Source of Cash (increase cash): decrease operating assets, increase operating liabilities
* Use of Cash (decrease cash): increase operating assets, decrease operating liabilities

Operating WC:
↑ Receivables → cash ↓
↑ Inventory → cash ↓
↑ Payables → cash ↑

↓ Receivables → cash ↑
↓ Inventory → cash ↑
↓ Payables → cash ↓

Cash from sales = Sales - Increase in A/R
(if this is the only relevant adjustment)

Free Cash Flow (FCF) is cash available for distribution to investors after the firm pays for its investments or addition to working capital.

FCF = Net Income + Interest (money paid to debt investors) + Depreciation - Addition to operating WC + Cash Flow from Investments (CapEx, negative sign already) = Cash Flow from Operations + Interest + Cash Flow from Investments

For FCF / operating cash flow:
ΔOperating NWC
= ΔReceivables + ΔInventory + ΔOther Operating Current Assets
- ΔAccounts Payable - ΔOther Operating Current Liabilities

Exclude:
* Cash / marketable securities
* Short-term debt / debt due for repayment



All equity financing => no debt => no interest expense => FCF = Cash Flow from Operations + Cash Flow from Investments

## Financial Statement Analysis

Market value metrics:
* Market Capitalization = (#share) x (share price)
* Market Value Added (MVA) = Market Value of Equity - Book Value of Equity
* Market-to-Book Ratio = Market Value of Equity / Book Value of Equity

Drawbacks: 
* reflect investors' expectations, not actual performance
* market value fluctuates with market conditions, not just company performance
* you can't look up market value of privately owned companies, nor the market value of divisions or plants of a company

### Economic Value Added (EVA)

Economic Value Added (EVA): After-Tax Operating Income minus a charge for the cost of capital employed; also called residual income.

* Residual Income: net dollar return after deducing the cost of capital

EVA = After-Tax Operating Income - (Cost of Capital x Total Capitalization)

* After-Tax Operating Income: firm's earning if 100% equity financed, = EBIT x (1 - Tax Rate) or = Net Income + Interest Expense x (1 - Tax Rate)

* Total Capitalization = Long-term debt + Shareholders' Equity (doesn't include other long-term liabilities) = Total Assets - Current Liabilities - (Other long-term liabilities)

EVA uses:
* After-tax operating income for the year
* Start-of-year total capitalization
* Cost of capital given for that year

Note: If tax rate is not provided or cannot be deduced, you may assume it as the current US corporate tax rate of 21%.

Accounting Rates of Return (ROR):
* Return on Capital (ROC) = After-Tax Operating Income / Total Capitalization
* Return on Assets (ROA) = After-Tax Operating Income / Total Assets
* Return on Equity (ROE) = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity

Denominator timing:
* Use start-of-year values if asked.
* Use average balance-sheet values if asked:
  Average = (Start + End) / 2.
* Always follow the wording of the question.

ROE measures return to equity, ROC and ROA measure the return to all investors, including interest paid as well as net income to shareholders.

EVA:
Advantages:
* Recognizes opportunity cost of capital
* Gives managers a clear cost-of-capital target
* Can improve by eliminating unproductive assets

Limitations:
* Based on book values
* Historical book values may poorly reflect current asset values
* Focuses on current accounting performance rather than market valuation

### Measuring Efficiency

Asset Turnover Ratio = Sales / Total Assets at start of the year or = Sales / Average total assets (start + end of year / 2) = For every dollar of assets, how many dollars of sales are generated

Inventory Turnover Ratio = COGS / Inventory at start of the year = How many times inventory is sold and replaced over a period of time (a year)

Average days in inventory = Inventory at start of the year / (COGS / 365) = 365 / Inventory Turnover Ratio = How many days on average it takes to sell the inventory

Receivables Turnover Ratio = Sales / Receivables at start of the year = How many times receivables are collected over a period of time (a year)

Average Collection Period = Receivables at start of the year / (Sales / 365) = 365 / Receivables Turnover Ratio = How many days on average it takes to collect the receivables

### The DuPont System

Profit Margin = Net Income / Sales = For every dollar of sales, how many dollars of profit are generated

Operating Profit Margin = After-Tax Operating Income / Sales = For every dollar of sales, how many dollars of operating profit are generated

ROA = After-Tax Operating Income / Total Assets = Assets Turnover Ratio x Operating Profit Margin = (Sales / Total Assets) x (After-Tax Operating Income / Sales)

Vertical Merger: When a firm acquires a supplier, the supplier’s sales to the firm become internal and are no longer counted as external sales. The combined firm keeps the supplier’s profit, which can improve operating profit margin, but it also takes on the supplier’s assets, which may reduce asset turnover. Therefore, the overall effect on ROA is uncertain.

### Measuring Financial Leverage

Long-term debt ratio = Long-term debt / (Long-term debt + Equity)

Long-term debt-equity ratio = Long-term debt / Equity

Total debt ratio = Total liabilities / Total assets

Times Interest Earned (TIE) = EBIT / Interest Payments

Cash coverage ratio = (EBIT + Depreciation) / Interest Payments

ROE = Leverage Ratio x Asset Turnover Ratio x Operating Profit Margin x Debt Burden Ratio = (Total Assets / Equity) x (Sales / Total Assets) x (After-Tax Operating Income / Sales) x (Net Income / After-Tax Operating Income)

Lower interest rate → Lower interest expense → Higher TIE and Cash Coverage → Higher Debt Burden Ratio → Higher ROE

### Measuring Liquidity

Net working capital to total assets ratio = NWC / Total Assets

Current ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Quick ratio = (Cash + marketable securities + Receivables) / Current Liabilities

Cash ratio = (Cash + marketable securities) / Current Liabilities

Liquidity transaction effects:

Cash used to pay A/P:
CA ↓, CL ↓ by same amount
→ NWC unchanged
→ if Current Ratio > 1, Current Ratio ↑

Cash used to buy Inventory:
CA unchanged
→ Current Ratio unchanged
→ Quick Ratio ↓
